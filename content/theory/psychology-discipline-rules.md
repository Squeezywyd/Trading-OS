> **Key Point:** Most losses come from breaking rules, not from the model. Track process first, P&L second — a losing trade taken correctly is a good trade; a winning trade taken by breaking a rule is a bad habit that happened to pay out this time.

## Why this needs rules, not willpower

Discipline is a system, not a character trait you either have or don't. After a real loss, the body reacts to the threat much like it would to physical danger: cortisol rises, activity in the amygdala (the fast, fight-or-flight part of the brain) increases, and activity in the prefrontal cortex — the part responsible for weighing consequences and sticking to a plan — measurably drops. That's not a mood, it's a temporary drop in decision-making capacity. The rules below exist precisely because willpower is the wrong tool to fight a biological response; a pre-committed rule you follow mechanically doesn't require willpower in the moment at all.

This matters directly for this app: most execution failures — moving a stop, oversizing, skipping confirmation, entering against bias — are logged as `mistakes` in the Trade Journal for a reason. They aren't random; they cluster right after losses and right after big wins, which is exactly what the science above predicts.

## Pre-session state check

- [ ] Slept enough, no hangover from the last session
- [ ] Daily Prep done before the killzone
- [ ] Risk rules and daily stop written
- [ ] No open emotional trigger (argument, money stress, distraction)

Any box unchecked: half size or no trade. This isn't a mood questionnaire — a genuine "no" on any of these measurably raises the odds of a rule break today.

## Revenge trading and tilt — what's actually happening

Revenge trading is the impulse to immediately recoup a loss, usually by sizing up and skipping confirmation, while the nervous system is still in the post-loss threat state described above. It is the single fastest way to turn one acceptable loss into an account-ending day. Three things make it less likely:

1. **Small per-trade risk.** When one trade only risks a small, pre-decided slice of the account, the loss doesn't register as threatening enough to trigger the full stress response in the first place — this is a second, psychological reason (beyond math) that the sizing formula in Risk Management & Prop Rules matters.
2. **A forced cooling-off period.** What you do in the first fifteen to sixty minutes after a real loss decides the rest of the day far more than the loss itself did. A hard, pre-committed break — not a "I'll trade if a good setup shows up" break — is the rule, because "a good setup" always seems to show up when you're looking for a reason to get back in.
3. **Logging the emotional state before, not after.** Tagging emotion in the journal in the moment (see the tag list below) creates the same pause that stops the impulsive click — the act of naming the state is itself a brake on acting from it.

## Tilt triggers (stop trading)

- Two losses in a row: 15 minute break, then re-read today's bias from Daily Prep before considering another trade.
- A loss that hits your daily stop: done for the day, full stop — not "one more to get back to even."
- Entering without a completed sweep + MSS (i.e. skipping the Trigger section of the A+ Setup Checklist).
- Sizing up after a loss, for any reason.
- Moving or removing a stop once it's placed.

## Emotion tags to use in the journal

`Calm`, `Confident`, `FOMO`, `Revenge`, `Fear`, `Hesitant`, `Overconfident` — these are the exact tags the Trade Journal tracks. Tag honestly, including on winners: an `Overconfident` win and a `Revenge` win are both warning signs even though the P&L looks identical. The Weekly Recap groups trades by emotion specifically so a pattern like "every `FOMO`-tagged trade this month lost" becomes visible instead of buried in a scroll of individual entries.

## Post-trade loop

1. Screenshot the setup before entry and after exit.
2. Grade the setup (A+ to C) against the checklist **before** looking at P&L — grading after you know the outcome quietly rewrites the grade to match the result.
3. Answer three questions: did I follow the plan? Was direction aligned with bias? What was the one mistake, if any?
4. Write one Lesson line — one sentence, specific enough that reading it cold in a month still means something.

## Weekly loop

- Count rule breaks for the week. Target zero, and treat any non-zero week as the primary agenda item for the next Weekly Recap — not the P&L.
- Compare A+ trade expectancy to B/C trade expectancy (see Risk Management & Prop Rules for the expectancy formula). If B/C setups are net negative, that's not a psychology problem — it's a signal to stop taking them, independent of how they feel to take.
- Pick exactly one focus for next week. Not three. A list of five things to fix is a list of zero things that get fixed.
